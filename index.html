Industrial psychology meets data engineering

The cloud failed in physical ways. Nobody could see whether the people running it were stretched thin.

So I built a company where I could. I hid the answer inside a year of messy data, then made my own models try to find it. The project grades itself, including where it gets things wrong.

What actually happened

Forrester saw it coming. Then 2026 happened.

In its Predictions 2026 research, Forrester said to expect at least two major, multiday outages at the big cloud providers. The reason it gave was money. Budget was moving out of older infrastructure and into GPU data centers built for AI.

October 2025 · AWSA DNS fault around DynamoDB in US-EAST-1 caused knock-on trouble for most of a day.
October 2025 · AzureAzure Front Door went down for about 8 hours.
February 2026 · AzureA regional problem in West US ran past 20 hours.
7 to 8 May 2026 · AWSA data hall in US-EAST-1 lost cooling. Full recovery took between 19 and 28 hours, depending on whose clock you use.
15 July 2026 · Google CloudA utility power fault took out cooling at a site in the Netherlands for roughly 12 to 15 hours.
20 August 2026 · Google CloudPlanned fiber maintenance went wrong in us-west1 and automatic rerouting failed. 33 services were hit for 2 hours 22 minutes.

Dates and durations come from outage trackers and trade press. The providers' own post-incident reports are the primary record. Whether this adds up to Forrester's "multiday" is a fair argument.

The thing nobody publishes. Every reported cause on that list is physical or procedural: cooling, power, cables, maintenance. Nobody published how many engineers were on shift, how much overtime they were working, or whether the ticket backlog was growing. That data is private, so from the outside you cannot ask whether stretched teams make failures more likely.
What I am not claiming. I am not saying tired engineers caused any of these outages. The reported causes were physical. I am asking a narrower question: if a company wanted to watch this risk build inside its own teams, what would the data look like, and could it be seen early enough to matter?

This is the third project in a line. Aegis asked whether low attrition after AI-linked layoffs means a healthy team or just people with nowhere to go. Vanguard asked whether a model can find a hidden tipping point and grade itself. Horizon puts them together.

The fictional company

So I built Horizon.

Horizon Infrastructure Group runs 28 clusters in 4 regions, looked after by 8 site reliability teams, over one simulated year from October 2025 to September 2026. It is fictional, which means I control everything about it, including the answer.

Region 1

na-east

Starts moving budget into AI in October to December 2025. First to be squeezed.

Region 2

eu-west

Starts in January to March 2026.

Region 3

apac-south

Starts in April to June 2026.

Control

latam-central

Never moves any budget. If it ever sets off an alarm, the method is broken.

After a region starts, 5 more points of its total budget move to AI each quarter, up to 20. Core maintenance begins at 60% of the budget, so the funded team shrinks as that share does. Hiring freezes in a diverting region: a leaver is replaced at 8% of the gap per week instead of 30%. Meanwhile demand grows 8% over the year, plus extra load for every point of budget moved to AI.

Four things I buried in it

The tipping point

0.72

Outage risk stays flat until a team's staffing falls below 72% of what its workload needs, then climbs over the next 10 points.

The delay

6 weeks

Risk responds to how stretched the team was six weeks ago, not today. Strain wears people down gradually.

The quiet teams

35% vs 5%

Teams that feel unsafe skip the ticket for 35% of real incidents. Teams that feel safe skip 5%.

A rival suspect

Wear

Deferred maintenance from the budget cuts also raises outages, so people are not the only possible explanation.

The psychology under the numbers

None of the human side is guesswork.

It rests on established theory from my own field. Each framework became a specific mechanism in the simulation, and the analysis then asks whether the data can pick that mechanism up.

Demerouti, Bakker and colleagues, 2001

Job Demands-Resources

Strain builds when demands rise and the resources to meet them shrink. In Horizon, demand is the AI-driven workload and resources are funded headcount. Their ratio is the capacity-to-load ratio, and overtime is the strain signal.

Hobfoll, 1989

Conservation of Resources

People protect what they have, losses hurt more than gains help, and losses feed on themselves. In Horizon, strain makes people leave, and every departure makes the remaining load heavier. That loop is built in.

Edmondson, 1999

Psychological safety

Whether people feel safe admitting problems. Every Horizon team has a hidden safety level, and low-safety teams skip tickets. A monthly pulse survey measures it, with noise.

Brockner and colleagues, 1980s

Layoff survivors

The people who stay carry extra load and worry. Hiring freezes and cuts start the moment a region begins moving budget, so the team that remains is smaller while demand grows.

Two design choices came straight from this work. Strain wears people down, so outages respond weeks later and not at once. And losses tend to matter most past a critical point, so I used a threshold with a ramp and not a straight line. The numbers themselves, 0.72 and six weeks, are my assumptions, chosen to be plausible.

What is going on, and what to do about it

When a region moves budget into AI, the funded team gets smaller while the workload gets bigger. Staffing relative to demand falls. The first signs are overtime and a growing ticket backlog. Then people leave, and each departure loads the rest. About six weeks after a team crosses the line, real outages start to climb. And the teams under the most strain, if they also feel unsafe, report less, so their ticket queue looks calmer than the truth.

Signal

Watch the leading indicators

Capacity against demand, overtime and backlog moved weeks before outages did. Track them, not just outage counts.

Signal

Overtime is a trigger

Sustained overtime above a team's normal is a warning, not proof of commitment.

Action

Backfill fast where load rises

A hiring freeze does the most damage on the teams whose demand is growing fastest.

Action

Do not trust ticket counts

Detect outages from system data. Use blameless post-mortems so reporting is low-risk. Fewer tickets can mean less safety.

Action

Move budget in steps

Re-measure capacity before each step, and keep a protected comparison group.

Two levers

Demands down or resources up

Narrower scope and lighter on-call, or more people, better tooling and real support. Doing neither while cutting maintenance is the option the model says will fail.

This shows the warning signals are detectable weeks ahead. It does not prove that acting on them would prevent a real outage. That would take real data and a proper intervention study.

The engineering

The data fights back.

Real companies do not hand you clean tables. Each of Horizon's six sources arrives at its own pace with its own flaws, and a ledger records exactly what I broke so the cleaning can be graded.

Telemetry · near real time

723 exact duplicates, 494 restated readings, 1,198 missing, 472 sensor glitches, 4,974 arriving hours late. And 60,999 rows from apac-south logged in local time as if it were UTC.

The monitors themselves

210 hours lost during severe outages, because monitoring goes quiet exactly when you need it most.

Staffing · weekly, days late

Team names spelled three ways, 13 missing weeks, restated rows, and -1 standing in for missing overtime.

Budget · quarterly

Money stored as text like $12.50M, and some quarters restated 45 days later.

Tickets · daily batch

One region stamped with a -03:00 offset, 68 duplicates, 190 blank severities, and root causes typed as free text.

Pulse survey · monthly

Scores hidden for any team with fewer than five responses, and noisy for the rest.

A medallion pipeline

LandingThe messy files, exactly as each system sends them.
BronzeTaken in as delivered, with load metadata. Refuses any answer-key table.
SilverCleaned. Timezone found from the data, duplicates removed, labels fixed.
Gold245,280 cluster-hours and 416 team-weeks, joined as of delivery date.
SQL and modelsDuckDB checks, three models, then grading against the hidden answer.

Time travel is not allowed

The slowest sources describe the past and arrive late. A careless join would hand a model facts the company did not yet know, and the results would look brilliant for the wrong reason. So Horizon joins on the day each record was delivered, not the day it describes. When two versions of a week exist, the newest week wins. Try it: move the date and see what the company actually knew.

A test checks 150 random points against a brute-force lookup.

One team's headcount

Three HR records. Slide "today".
Strict as-of joins

Staffing, budget and survey data only ever show what was known at that moment. No row sees its own future.

A SQL layer

DuckDB holds the gold tables. Three SQL files compute the capacity index with window functions and run 10 data-quality checks. A test confirms SQL and Python agree to within one billionth.

96 tests

Warnings count as errors, and the whole suite runs on every push. It takes about 15 seconds.

Guard rails

Tests fail if the pipeline or the models import the simulator or read the answer folder. The pipeline also runs 17 checks of its own every time.

Fast by design

Vectorized, with no row-by-row loops. The in-memory run from bronze to gold over 245,280 rows takes about a third of a second.

No black boxes

The models are plain NumPy: a Poisson regression with grid search and profile-likelihood intervals. No machine-learning libraries.

The honest part

Where it got it wrong.

The tipping point. The model put it at 0.81. The truth was 0.72.

It is the weak spot, and I would rather show you than hide it. The analysis cannot see true workload, so CPU use stands in for it. That stand-in drifts, and the interval it produces was too narrow in half of my simulated worlds. The tipping point landed within 0.05 of the truth in 5 of 8 worlds.

Early warning has a softer flaw too. Its threshold is estimated from the whole year, which makes the backtest more generous than a live system would be.

Everything here is synthetic. It says nothing about any real company, and nothing about what caused any real outage.

Taller is a better fit. Anything near the top cannot be told apart.
